      How good is the forecast?

      A forecast is only useful if you know how far to trust it. NESO publishes no accuracy figure for its own carbon forecast, so Gridcast keeps every forecast as it was issued and checks it against what happened. This page grows as that record does.

      What is known so far

      A pilot compared NESO's national forecast with its national actual for every half hour of 2025.

      These figures probably flatter the forecast. The API keeps one forecast for each past half hour and does not say when it was made, so most of these errors come from forecasts made close to the time. Gridcast's own archive records when every forecast was issued, which is what makes the error a day or two ahead measurable.

      Four tests, fixed before the data

      Each claim was written down with the result that would disprove it before any of the data that tests it existed.

      1. H1 Gridcast's weather-driven model beats simple baselines. Disproved if repeating yesterday, or the average of the last four weeks, is as accurate at any forecast horizon. Not yet testedDue in week 8 of 12.
      2. H2 NESO's forecast gets worse the further ahead it looks. Disproved if its error 24 to 48 hours ahead is not clearly larger than 1 to 6 hours ahead. Not yet testedDue in week 6 of 12.
      3. H3 A day or two ahead, Gridcast is as accurate as NESO. Disproved if NESO's error is clearly lower 24 to 48 hours ahead. Not yet testedDue in week 9 of 12.
      4. H4 The recommended window is close to the best one. Disproved if, on fewer than 70 in 100 days, the three hours recommended at 18:00 the evening before come within 10% of that day's cleanest three hours. Not yet testedDue in week 10 of 12.

      What these numbers cannot tell you

      There is no published actual for each region, only for Great Britain as a whole. Regional forecasts are checked against NESO's latest estimate for the half hour, which is itself a model, so the verdicts above rest on the national comparison.

      The figures are for average carbon intensity. Using electricity at a cleaner time adds load that the grid meets from whichever plant is on the margin, which can be dirtier than the average, so a cleaner window does not mean an equal cut in your emissions.

      The first verdicts will come from autumn and winter. Solar power moves the cleanest hours towards midday in summer, so they should be repeated after a full year.
